On December 11, 1940 the Japanese attacked the US naval base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii. This event was used by the President, Franklin Roosevelt, to enter the US into World War II. Owen Josephus Roberts was an associate justice of the United States Supreme Court from 1930 to 1945.[1] He also led the Roberts Commissions, which investigated the attack on Pearl Harbor. Some claimed that the report also concluded that both Japanese diplomats and persons of Japanese ancestry had engaged in widespread espionage leading up to the attack. Despite the fact that the report made no mention of Americans of Japanese ancestry, the media, as well as politicians like California Governor Culbert L. Olson, nevertheless used it to vilify Japanese Americans and inflame public opinion against them.

Executine Order 9066 created the Military Exclusion Zones, from which Japanese-Americans were removed and relocated. About 125,284 people of Japanese descent were relocated to 75 camps, which were built in remote locations.

The evacuation orders often came with days notice, with most unable to sell off posessions that could not be carried. Homes, businesses, and personal posessions were left behind, never to be recovered.  These citizens were taken to "relocation centers", or incarceration caamps. These camps were "not prisons", even though they were surrounded by barbed wire, and guard towers, whose weapons were pointed inward.

Some Japanese-American men were taken to prisoner-of-war camps, while many were taken to camps that housed entire families. The camps were harsh. The prisoners often had to build all structures in the camps.

As Japanese-American men tried to enlist, they were refused. Later in the war, when the US waas desperate for soldiers, they were allowed to enlist. This was the 442nd Regiment, which became the most decorated in U.S. military history.

One Japanese-American citizen, Fred Korematsu, fought the act that created the concentration camps. This fight went all the way to the Supreme Court, as Korematsu v. United States. In a 6-3 decision the Court deferred to the US military, and ruled to uphold the law. One of the three dissenters, was Justice Roberts, who wrote that "it is the case of convicting a citizen as a punishment for not submitting to imprisonment in a concentration camp, based on his ancestry, and solely because of his ancestry." This case was reopened in 1983, with evidence previously withheld, and overturned.

Within two years if the initial ruling, World War 2 had ended and the camps were no longer necessary. The Japanese-Amercans, still facing the fallout from the war, and racism within the US, had to re-integrate back into society. Even though no Japanese-Americans had been convicted of sabotage or any of the other fears the US used to justify thier incarceration, they were often still viewed by some as Un-American. This is a sentiment still faced by Americans of Asian descent today.
